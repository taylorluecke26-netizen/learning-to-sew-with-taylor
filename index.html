getting acquainted

Meet your machine

Every home machine, however basic, shares the same core parts. Learn these once and you can sit at almost any machine in the world.

2.1 The parts, labeled

#PartWhat it does
1Spool pinHolds your thread spool as it feeds into the machine.
2Tension dialControls how tightly the top thread pulls. Leave on the default setting unless stitches look loopy or puckered.
3NeedleCarries the top thread down through the fabric, meeting the bobbin thread underneath.
4Presser footHolds fabric flat against the feed dogs. Always lower it before stitching.
5BobbinA small spool under the needle plate supplying the bottom thread.
6Foot pedalControls speed, like a car's gas pedal. Press gently.

2.2 Threading, in order

Follow your Dechow manual's bobbin-winding diagram, then drop the wound bobbin into its case so thread unwinds counterclockwise.

This releases the tension discs so thread seats properly. Turn the hand wheel toward you until the needle is at its highest point.

Spool pin → thread guide → tension dial → take-up lever → needle. Your manual shows this as a numbered diagram — follow it once and it becomes muscle memory.

Hold the top thread, turn the hand wheel one full rotation, and a loop of bobbin thread appears through the needle plate. Pull both threads back under the presser foot.

Stitch a straight line on spare fabric first. Even stitches with no bunching underneath means you're ready.

If stitches bunch up underneathStop, cut the threads, and rethread from the very top — most tangles trace back to a skipped threading step, not a broken machine.

the dictionary

Sewing speak, decoded

Pattern instructions read like a foreign language until you know these terms. This is the page every clickable pink word links back to.

Fabric & cutting
Right side / wrong sideThe "right" side is the fabric's public-facing side. Sheets often look the same both ways — mark one side with a chalk dot to keep track.
GrainlineThe arrow printed on a pattern piece. It must run parallel to the fabric's selvage so finished pants hang straight instead of twisting.
SelvageThe tightly woven, non-fraying edge running along the fabric's long sides — here, the sheet's long hemmed edges.
NotchesLittle triangles on a pattern's cut edge. Match notch to notch between pieces so you sew the right sections together in the right order.
Sewing & construction
Right sides together (RST)Stack two pieces with their right sides facing each other, so once stitched and flipped, the seam is hidden inside.
Seam allowanceThe strip of fabric between your stitch line and the cut edge — S1563 uses 5/8 inch unless noted otherwise.
BackstitchA few reverse stitches at the start and end of a seam that lock the thread so it can't unravel.
BasteA loose, temporary stitch used to hold something in place before final stitching.
TopstitchA visible line of stitching on the right side of the fabric, for looks and reinforcement.
UnderstitchStitching that anchors a facing or seam allowance flat so it doesn't roll to the front.
Ease / gatheringDistributing slightly more fabric across a shorter stitch line so two pieces match up smoothly.
Waistband & finishing
CasingA folded-over tunnel of fabric, stitched down, that elastic or a drawstring threads through.
BodkinA blunt tool (or a safety pin in a pinch) used to feed elastic or a drawstring through a casing.
ButtonholeA small reinforced slit stitched by the machine, sized to let a drawstring or cord pass through the outside of the casing.
The machine
Spool pinThe post that holds your thread spool as it feeds into the machine.
TensionHow tightly the top thread pulls as it stitches. Leave on default unless stitches look loopy or puckered.
Presser footThe metal foot that holds fabric flat against the feed dogs.
Feed dogsSmall metal teeth under the presser foot that pull fabric through as you sew.
BobbinA small spool under the needle plate supplying the bottom thread.

reading the envelope

Cracking open Simplicity S1563

Spend five minutes reading the envelope back before unfolding the tissue — it answers most beginner questions before you ask them.

4.1 What's printed on the envelope

Size chart

Ignore your usual clothing size. Measure waist and hip in inches and match to the pattern's own chart.

Views

Different length or detail options. Pick the same view for both of you so you only learn one process.

Yardage

How much fabric each size needs — compare it to your sheet's dimensions before cutting.

Notions

Everything besides fabric: thread, elastic or cord width. Your kit list, confirmed.

4.2 Take two measurements

MeasurementHow to take it
WaistTape measure around the natural waistline, snug but not tight
HipAround the fullest part of the hips/seat, a few inches below the waist

Take his measurements the same way. Since these pants have a flexible waist finish, you have a lot of forgiving room — when in doubt, size up.

4.3 Inside the tissue envelope

The instruction sheet lists which numbered tissue pieces you need for pants. Ignore pieces for other views.

Multi-size patterns print several nested cutting lines. Cut along the line matching your measured size, one set for you, one for him.

A cool, dry iron on low removes fold creases so pieces lie flat against your fabric later.

Short on time?Skip reading the full instruction sheet and go straight to "Sewing the pants" — it translates the steps into plain language, in the order you'll actually do them.

one sheet, two pairs

Prepping and cutting your fabric

A flat sheet is already hemmed on all four sides, so it needs a little extra prep before it behaves like yardage.

Fabric shrinks the first time it's washed. Do it now so your finished pants don't shrink out of fit later.

A wrinkled base layer throws off cutting accuracy. Iron the whole sheet flat.

Cut away the sheet's finished hem and any elastic corner pockets — you only want the flat, open middle.

Most pieces are cut on the fold so left and right legs come out as mirror images.

Arrange every tissue piece for the larger size first, following each grainline arrow, before pinning or cutting anything.

Pin within the seam allowance, every 4-6 inches, smoothing as you go.

Cut through both pattern tissue and fabric together in one motion, for a clean edge.

Snip tiny inward triangles at each notch mark, or dot them with chalk.

Grainline checkLay a tape measure from the grainline arrow to the fabric's selvage at both ends. If the distances don't match, the piece is crooked — nudge it until they're equal.
Why a sheet is a smart first fabricSewing creators like Sewing with Solana often steer beginners toward soft, stable wovens like cotton or poplin — a brushed microfiber sheet behaves the same way, staying put under the presser foot while you're still learning to guide it.

the main event

Sewing the pants, step by step

This order applies to both pairs. Sew hers completely first as your practice run, then repeat on his.

Straight stitch, length 2.5mm5/8" seam allowance

6.1 Build the tube

With right sides together, pin front-to-front and back-to-back along the curved crotch seam, matching notches. Stitch, backstitching at both ends.

Open the two pieces from step 1 into one tube shape. Pin right sides together along both outer leg seams and stitch top to bottom.

With the pants still inside out, bring the two inner leg edges together and stitch from one ankle opening up through the crotch to the other ankle.

Turn the pants right side out and run a warm iron along each seam — this habit separates homemade-looking projects from polished ones.

6.2 Finish the waist — pick your path

You don't need elastic to make these work. Choose whichever you have on hand.

Fold the top edge over by about 1.5 inches, press, then stitch close to the folded raw edge, leaving a 2 inch gap unstitched to feed elastic through.

Attach a safety pin (or bodkin) to one end of your elastic and feed it through the casing tunnel, scrunching fabric along as you go. Overlap the two ends by an inch, stitch them together firmly, then close the gap in the casing.

Community trickBefore you stitch the elastic ends together, run a finger all the way around the casing to confirm it isn't twisted — much easier to fix now than after it's closed.

Same as the elastic version — fold the top edge over by about 1.5 inches, press, and stitch close to the folded raw edge, all the way around (no gap needed this time).

Before threading anything, mark two small buttonholes on the outside of the pants, on either side of center front, right at the casing. Set your machine's buttonhole foot to match your drawstring's thickness — test on a scrap first.

Use cord, ribbon, or twill tape — or make one from a fabric scrap: cut a long strip, fold it in half lengthwise, stitch the long edge, then turn it right-side out.

Attach a safety pin to one end and feed it in through one buttonhole, all the way around the casing, and back out the same hole. Both ends now hang out the front.

Worth knowingA drawstring won't cinch itself the way elastic does — expect a bow tied each time. Some people split the difference: elastic in the back half of the waistband, drawstring through the front, so the pants stay up on their own but are still adjustable.

6.3 Hem the legs

Try the pants on (or hold them up to him). Fold the raw ankle edge under twice by about half an inch, press, and stitch close to the inner fold all the way around each leg.

Sewing order, pinnedCrotch seams → side seams → inseam → press → casing → elastic or drawstring → hem. Every pair of pull-on pants follows roughly this order.

when it doesn't go to plan

Troubleshooting the usual suspects

Every beginner hits these. None of them mean you're bad at this — they mean you're sewing.

What you seeLikely cause and fix
Thread bunches into a nest underneathAlmost always a threading error. Raise the presser foot, rethread the top completely, and re-seat the bobbin.
Machine skips stitchesOften a dull or bent needle. Swap in a fresh universal needle.
Fabric puckers along the seamTension is usually too tight, or you're pulling fabric through instead of guiding it. Loosen tension slightly.
Needle breaksUsually from pulling fabric sideways or sewing over a pin. Remove pins just before the needle reaches them.
Elastic twisted inside the casingUntwist by pulling it flat through the gap before stitching the ends together.
Seam looks crookedSeam ripper it out and re-sew. Two minutes now beats wearing a crooked seam as long as the pants last.
Two pieces don't match at the notchesOne piece is likely flipped. Unpin, flip it right-side-together correctly, recheck.

the last stretch

Finishing touches and care

A last full press, right side out, makes the finished pants look intentional rather than homemade.

Check inside and out for loose tails left from backstitching and trim them close to the fabric.

Check the waist sits comfortably and the hems fall where you want — small tweaks are still easy here.

A first gentle-cycle wash sets the stitches. After that, launder as you normally would.

That's two finished, matching pairs of pajama pants — and a machine you now know how to thread, tension, and troubleshoot from memory. 🎉
